Pay Less Notice Template (Construction Act)

What a pay less notice is, when you have to serve one, and what it must contain, explained in plain English, with an editable Word template.

What is a pay less notice?

A pay less notice is a written notice that the paying party on a construction contract gives when it intends to pay less than the sum that has been notified as due. It comes from section 111 of the Housing Grants, Construction and Regeneration Act 1996 (usually called the Construction Act), as amended by the Local Democracy, Economic Development and Construction Act 2009. The amended rules apply to construction contracts made on or after 1 October 2011 in England and Wales (1 November 2011 in Scotland).

The basic rule is simple: once a sum has been notified as due, the payer must pay that "notified sum" by the final date for payment, unless a valid pay less notice has been given in time. A main contractor paying a subcontractor, or a client paying a main contractor, is in exactly the same position.

Where does the notified sum come from?

  • Payment notice: the payer (or a named person such as a contract administrator) should issue a payment notice no later than five days after each payment due date, stating the sum considered due and how it was calculated.
  • Payee's notice: if the contract allows it, the payee's own application can stand as the payment notice.
  • Default payment notice: if the payer doesn't issue a payment notice, the payee can serve one, and the sum in it becomes the notified sum.

Whichever route applies, the pay less notice is the payer's only way to pay less than that figure without breaching the contract.

When must a pay less notice be served?

It must be given no later than the "prescribed period" before the final date for payment. The contract sets that period. If the contract's payment terms don't meet the Act's requirements, the Scheme for Construction Contracts fills the gap, and under the Scheme the notice must be given no later than seven days before the final date for payment. Standard forms often set their own period, for example some JCT forms use five days. Always check your contract's payment clauses and work out the dates for each payment cycle.

A notice served late is generally ineffective, and the full notified sum becomes payable. That is why most disputes about pay less notices turn on dates, so keep proof of when each notice was sent and received.

What must a pay less notice contain?

  • The sum the payer considers to be due on the date the notice is given.
  • The basis on which that sum is calculated, not just a headline figure. Itemise each deduction and the reason for it, such as defective work, incomplete work or contra-charges permitted by the contract.
  • Clear references: the parties, the contract, and the application or payment notice it responds to.
  • The date of the notice and who gives it.

The Act says the notice may be given by the payer or a specified person, and that a pay less notice can be combined with a payment notice in some circumstances. Keep it clear and specific. Vague notices that don't show how the figure was reached are a common reason for adjudicators to find against the payer.

What happens if one isn't served?

If no valid pay less notice is given, the payer must pay the notified sum by the final date for payment. If it doesn't, the payee may have the right to suspend performance after giving at least seven days' notice of its intention to suspend, and either party can refer a dispute to adjudication at any time. Disagreements about the true value of the work can still be raised, but generally only after the notified sum has been paid.

Does the Construction Act apply to my job?

Most contracts for construction operations in the UK are covered, including subcontracts and labour-only contracts. There are exclusions, most notably contracts with a residential occupier for work on their own home, and certain specialist operations listed in the Act. Employment contracts are not construction contracts. If you're unsure whether the Act applies, check before relying on it.
