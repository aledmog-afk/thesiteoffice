Variations & dayworks

Daywork Sheet Template

How to record labour, plant and materials on daywork so the cost can be verified and paid, with an editable Word daywork sheet.

What is a daywork sheet?

A daywork sheet (sometimes called a daywork voucher or record) records the actual resources used on a piece of work so it can be paid on a cost basis: who worked, for how many hours, what plant was used, and what materials went in. It is used when work can't sensibly be valued by measurement or by contract rates, which usually means variations, extra work, or work done in unusual conditions.

Dayworks are normally valued at the prime cost of labour, plant and materials, with percentage additions agreed in the contract. Many UK contracts refer to the RICS Definition of Prime Cost of Daywork carried out under a Building Contract to define what counts as prime cost. Check which definition and which percentages your contract uses.

When should you use one?

  • When you've been instructed to carry out extra or changed work, and the contract or the instruction says it will be valued on daywork.
  • When the conditions of the work have changed so much that measured rates no longer apply.
  • As back-up evidence for any variation, even where it may later be valued another way.

Dayworks are a valuation method, not an instruction. Before you start, make sure there is a written instruction or variation order for the work itself. A signed daywork sheet records the resources used; it doesn't usually prove that the work was instructed or that daywork is the agreed valuation method.

What a daywork sheet should record

  • Project, contract, and the instruction or variation reference the work relates to.
  • Date and a clear description of the work and where it was carried out.
  • Each operative's name, trade or grade, and hours worked, including start and finish times.
  • Plant used, with hours on hire or in use, and whether it was operated or not.
  • Materials used, with quantities, and delivery notes or invoices where available.
  • Signatures from your supervisor and from the client's or main contractor's representative, with their name and date.

When to submit it and who signs

Submit daywork sheets as quickly as possible, ideally getting them signed on the day the work is done. Standard forms commonly set a short deadline for submitting daywork records for verification (JCT forms, for example, typically require them by the end of the week after the work was carried out). Miss the deadline, or leave sheets unsigned, and the work can be very hard to recover.

The person signing on the other side is usually confirming that the time and resources recorded are accurate. Some representatives sign "for record purposes only", which means the valuation may still be argued later. That's another reason to keep the description specific and link each sheet to an instruction.

Agree the rates before you need them

Daywork disputes are often about rates, not hours. Check before work starts which labour rates, plant rates and percentage additions apply, whether they are set out in the contract or a schedule, and whether overtime, travel or small tools are included. If the contract is silent, agree a daywork schedule in writing early. Your sheets then only need to prove the quantities, and the money follows from the agreed rates.

Tips that help dayworks get paid

  • Number your sheets and keep a register so nothing gets lost.
  • Keep photos of the work before, during and after, dated and cross-referenced to the sheet.
  • Don't mix contract work and daywork on the same sheet.
  • Include dayworks in your next application for payment with copies of the signed sheets.
  • If a sheet is refused, record why in writing straight away.
