What is a concrete pour record?

A concrete pour record is the site's evidence of how a particular element was cast: that it was inspected before the pour, that the right concrete arrived and was placed within time, that tests and samples were taken, and how it was protected and cured. When a cube result comes back low, or a defect appears months later, the pour record is usually the first thing people ask to see.

There isn't a single law that prescribes a pour record. Requirements come from the project specification, the structural engineer's requirements and the inspection and test plan (ITP). Many main contractors expect one for every structural pour.

Before the pour

  • Element, gridlines and level, with the drawing references and revision.
  • Formwork checked: line, level, stability, cleanliness, release agent and joints.
  • Reinforcement checked: bar sizes, spacing, laps, fixings and cover, with spacers in place.
  • Cast-in items, ducts, sleeves and waterstops in position.
  • Hold points released: who inspected and signed off, and when.
  • Weather forecast checked, and protection ready for rain, heat or frost.

During the pour

  • Supplier, mix or specification reference (for example the designation or designed mix under BS 8500 / BS EN 206), and the specified consistence class.
  • For each truck: delivery ticket number, volume, time batched, time arrived and time discharge finished.
  • Consistence (slump) test results and any trucks rejected, with the reason.
  • Test cubes or cylinders taken: how many, from which truck, and their reference numbers.
  • Any water or admixture added on site, and who authorised it.
  • Placing and compaction method, any pump or skip used, and air and concrete temperatures.
  • Any stoppages, cold joints or problems, with times.

Specifications commonly set a maximum time between batching and full discharge, and limits on adding water on site. Check the specification for your project and record the times on every ticket, so you can show the concrete was placed within them.

After the pour

  • Finish achieved, and any defects noticed.
  • Curing method and duration, and protection used.
  • Striking times for formwork, and who approved them.
  • Cube test results when they come back (often at 7 and 28 days), recorded against the sample references.

Weather and temperature

Cold weather slows strength gain and can damage fresh concrete if it freezes. Hot, dry or windy weather speeds up setting and drying and increases the risk of cracking. The specification will usually set limits and protection measures for both. Recording air and concrete temperatures at the time of the pour, along with the protection used afterwards, shows those requirements were followed and helps explain any results that come back lower than expected.

Why the record matters later

If cube results fail, the engineer will want to know which trucks the samples came from, how the concrete was placed and compacted, how it was cured and whether anything unusual happened on the day. Without a pour record, the usual answer is more testing, such as cores taken from the finished element, which costs time and money and may lead to remedial work. A complete record also forms part of the quality and handover file for the client.

Tips for useful pour records

  • Attach the delivery tickets and photographs of the reinforcement before the pour.
  • Fill the record in during the pour, not afterwards from memory.
  • Use the same reference for the pour across the ITP, the pour record and the cube register.
  • Get the sign-off from the person who released the hold point.
